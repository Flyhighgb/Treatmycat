A diagnosis of kidney disease is hard to hear, but it is not the end of the road. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is one of the commonest illnesses in older cats, and while it can't be cured, it usually progresses slowly. With a kidney diet, good hydration and regular checkups, many cats have months or years of good-quality life after diagnosis. Your part is the daily care at home, and noticing early when something changes.

## Chronic or sudden?

Kidney problems in cats come in two very different forms, and the second is an emergency.

**Lilies are the classic danger.** True lilies (Easter, tiger, Asiatic, daylilies and others) can cause kidney failure in cats from a nibble of a leaf, a lick of pollen or a sip of vase water. If your cat may have touched one, go to a vet straight away, even before signs appear, and call the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center on (888) 426-4435 if you need advice on the way.

## Signs of kidney disease

Early CKD often shows no signs at all, which is why yearly blood and urine tests for older cats matter. As it progresses you may notice:

- Drinking more and peeing more (bigger clumps in the litter box)
- Weight loss and muscle wasting along the back
- Eating less, or becoming fussy about food
- Vomiting, sometimes with drooling or lip-licking from nausea
- Bad breath with an ammonia smell, or mouth ulcers
- A dull, scruffy coat
- Tiredness, weakness and hiding
- Constipation, often from dehydration

## How vets diagnose and stage it

Your vet will run **blood tests** (including creatinine and often SDMA, which can pick up kidney changes earlier), a **urine test** to check how concentrated the urine is and whether it contains protein or infection, and a **blood pressure** check. High blood pressure is common in cats with CKD and can damage the eyes, sometimes causing sudden blindness.

The results place your cat in one of four stages from the International Renal Interest Society (IRIS). Stage 1 is the mildest and stage 4 the most advanced. The stage guides which treatments your vet recommends and how often your cat should be rechecked.

Depending on the results, treatment may include:

- a **prescription kidney diet**
- medicine for **high blood pressure** or **protein in the urine**
- **phosphate binders** mixed into food
- **potassium** supplements
- treatment for **nausea**, **poor appetite** or **anemia**
- **fluids under the skin** that you learn to give at home

All of these are given as your vet prescribes, and doses often change as rechecks come in. If tablets are a struggle, our guide to [giving a cat a tablet](/guides/how-to-give-a-cat-a-tablet/) can help, and your vet may have liquid or flavored versions.

## Care at home

### Feed a kidney diet, slowly

Prescription kidney diets are lower in phosphorus and contain controlled amounts of good-quality protein. International Cat Care and Cornell describe diet as one of the most effective ways to slow CKD and help cats feel better. Switch over gradually across a few weeks, mixing it with the old food. If your cat refuses it, tell your vet rather than giving up: there are several brands and textures, and a cat that eats something is far better than one that eats nothing.

### Keep the water going in

Failing kidneys lose water, so hydration is a big part of care.

- Feed **wet food** where your cat accepts it, and add a little extra water.
- Put out **several water bowls** in different rooms, wide and shallow, refilled daily.
- Try a **water fountain**, since many cats drink more from moving water. [See cat water fountains](amazon:cat water fountain).
- If your vet prescribes **fluids under the skin**, ask the clinic to show you how, and do it in a calm, warm spot with a treat afterward.

### Protect the appetite

Nausea and a poor appetite are common. Warm wet food slightly, serve small, frequent meals, and keep bowls clean. Our guide to [getting a picky cat to eat](/guides/how-to-get-a-picky-cat-to-eat/) has more ideas. Tell your vet if appetite drops, because treating nausea often brings it back.

### Make life easy

Older cats with CKD often also have stiff joints. Keep food, water, beds and a low-sided litter box on one level, add a warm bed, and keep the litter box very clean, since your cat will use it more.

### Keep records and rechecks

Weigh your cat every couple of weeks if you can, note appetite and thirst, and keep every recheck appointment. Changes in weight or blood results often show up before your cat looks unwell.

## Don't

- Give any human painkiller. Acetaminophen (Tylenol) is deadly to cats, and ibuprofen, naproxen and aspirin can damage the kidneys.
- Give anti-inflammatory medicines (including leftover ones prescribed for pain) without checking with your vet that they are safe for a cat with CKD.
- Bring lilies into the house, or let your cat near antifreeze.
- Switch to home-cooked or "kidney support" supplements in place of your vet's plan.
- Change or stop prescribed medicine without talking to your vet.

## Call your vet promptly if

- Your cat stops eating for a day, or vomits more than once
- It seems very weak, wobbly or hides away
- It suddenly seems blind, bumps into things or has dilated pupils
- It pees much less, or strains in the litter box (see our [urinary tract guide](/guides/how-to-help-cat-urinary-tract-problem/))
- Gums look pale, or breath suddenly smells much worse
- It has a seizure or collapses (an emergency)

## Common questions

### How long can a cat live with kidney disease?
It varies a lot with the stage at diagnosis and how well a cat responds to treatment. Cats diagnosed early often live for years; in advanced stages it may be months. Your vet can give you the best idea for your cat.

### Is kidney disease painful for cats?
CKD itself isn't usually described as painful, but nausea, mouth ulcers, dehydration and constipation make cats feel unwell. Treating those is a big part of keeping your cat comfortable.

### Can I feed my cat with kidney disease regular food?
Kidney diets are recommended because they can slow the disease. If your cat won't eat one, talk to your vet about other kidney diets before going back to regular food, since eating something always comes first.

### Do all old cats get kidney disease?
No, but it becomes much more common with age. Yearly checkups with blood and urine tests from middle age onward help catch it early.

### How do I know when it's time to say goodbye?
When a cat stops eating, can't be kept comfortable, or has more bad days than good, talk honestly with your vet. A quality-of-life conversation is part of good care, not giving up.
