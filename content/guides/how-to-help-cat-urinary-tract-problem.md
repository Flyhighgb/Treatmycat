If your cat is straining in the litter box, peeing small amounts often, or peeing outside the box, it may have a urinary tract problem. Vets call this group of conditions **feline lower urinary tract disease (FLUTD)**. It's common and very treatable, but there is one situation that can't wait: **a cat that is straining and producing little or no urine may be blocked, and that is a life-threatening emergency**, especially in male cats. If in doubt, call your vet now.

## Signs of a urinary problem

- **Straining** in the litter box, or squatting for a long time
- Going back to the box **again and again**, passing only small amounts
- **Blood** in the urine, or pink-tinged clumps in the litter
- **Crying or yowling** while peeing
- **Peeing outside the litter box**, often on cool, smooth surfaces like tiles, baths or sinks
- **Licking the genital area** more than usual

## The emergency: a blocked bladder

Male cats have a long, narrow urethra that can be blocked by crystals, a plug of mucus or a tiny stone. Urine then can't get out, and toxins and potassium build up in the blood. Without treatment, a fully blocked cat can die within a day or two.

**Go to your vet or an emergency clinic straight away** if your cat:

- Is straining and passing **no urine or only drops**
- **Cries out** in the litter box or when its belly is touched
- Is **vomiting**, hiding, lethargic or not eating along with any urinary signs
- Has a **hard, swollen belly**

It's easy to confuse a blocked cat with a constipated one, because both strain in the box. Check the litter for urine clumps. If you're not sure which it is, treat it as an emergency. (For constipation itself, see our guide to [helping a constipated cat](/guides/how-to-treat-cat-constipation/).)

## Common causes

- **Feline idiopathic cystitis (FIC).** The most common cause in young and middle-aged cats. "Idiopathic" means there's no single cause, but **stress** plays a big part, along with not drinking enough. It tends to flare up and settle down again.
- **Bladder stones or crystals**, such as struvite or calcium oxalate. Diet and urine concentration affect how these form.
- **Urethral plugs**, a mix of crystals, mucus and cells that can block male cats.
- **Bacterial urinary tract infections.** These are less common in young cats but more likely in older cats, especially those with kidney disease, diabetes or hyperthyroidism.
- **Less often**, tumors or injuries.

Risk factors include being male, overweight, middle-aged, indoor-only, inactive, fed mostly dry food, and living in a stressful household, such as one with conflict between cats.

## What your vet will do

Your vet will examine your cat, feel its bladder, and usually test a urine sample. They may also suggest X-rays or an ultrasound to look for stones. Treatment depends on the cause. It can include pain relief, a prescription urinary diet to dissolve certain stones or prevent crystals, antibiotics if there's an infection, or a procedure under anesthesia to unblock a blocked cat. Follow your vet's plan, and give any medicines exactly as prescribed.

## How to help at home and prevent flare-ups

Once your vet has checked your cat, these steps help most cats with urinary problems, especially FIC.

1. **Get more water into your cat.** This is the most important step. Diluted urine is less likely to form crystals and less irritating to the bladder. Feed wet food for some or all meals, add a little water to meals, and offer several water bowls around the house. Many cats drink more from a fountain ([see cat water fountains](amazon:cat water fountain)).
2. **Use the food your vet recommends.** If your vet prescribes a urinary diet, feed it consistently. Mixing in other foods or treats can stop it working.
3. **Get the litter boxes right.** Have at least one more box than you have cats, in quiet places on every floor. Scoop at least once a day and use unscented, fine-grained litter. A large, uncovered box suits most cats ([see large litter boxes](amazon:extra large cat litter box)).
4. **Reduce stress.** Keep a predictable routine. Give each cat its own food, water, resting and hiding spots. Provide high places to climb and daily play sessions (see our guide to [the best toys for indoor cats](/guides/best-toys-for-indoor-cats/)). Make changes such as new pets or moving house gradually.
5. **Help your cat stay at a healthy weight** with measured meals and play.
6. **Watch the litter box.** Learn what normal looks like, so you notice changes in the number or size of urine clumps early.

## Never

- **Wait and see with a straining male cat.** A blockage can become fatal fast.
- **Give human painkillers.** Acetaminophen (Tylenol) is deadly to cats, and ibuprofen and aspirin can cause serious harm.
- **Use leftover antibiotics** or another pet's medicine. Most cases of FLUTD aren't caused by infection, and the wrong medicine can delay the right treatment.
- **Use cranberry products or home remedies to "flush" the bladder** in place of a vet visit. There's little evidence they help cats, and they can delay diagnosis.
- **Punish a cat for peeing outside the box.** It's usually a sign of pain or stress, and punishment makes stress worse.

## See a vet if

- Your cat shows **any** urinary signs, even if it's still peeing. FLUTD is painful, and early treatment helps.
- Signs **keep coming back** after treatment
- Your cat is **drinking or peeing much more** than usual. This can be a sign of kidney disease, diabetes or hyperthyroidism.

## Common questions

### Why is my cat peeing outside the litter box?

It's often a sign of a urinary problem or pain, so start with a vet check. If your cat is healthy, the cause may be the litter box itself (dirty, too small, in a busy spot) or stress, such as conflict with another cat.

### Can female cats get blocked?

It's much rarer, because females have a wider urethra. But female cats get cystitis, stones and infections too, and need a vet if they show urinary signs.

### Can FLUTD come back?

Yes. Idiopathic cystitis often flares up again, especially at stressful times. Keeping your cat well hydrated and reducing stress lowers the chance of it returning.

### Is dry food bad for cats with urinary problems?

Dry food isn't harmful in itself, but cats on dry food alone tend to have more concentrated urine. For cats with urinary problems, vets usually recommend adding wet food or water, or a prescription urinary diet.

### How long does a cystitis flare-up last?

Many flare-ups of idiopathic cystitis settle within a few days to a week. But because the signs look the same as a blockage or stones, every flare-up needs a vet check, not just waiting it out.

## The short version

Straining with little or no urine, especially in a male cat, is an emergency. For other urinary signs, see your vet soon. To help prevent flare-ups, get more water into your cat, feed the diet your vet recommends, keep litter boxes clean and plentiful, and reduce stress at home.
