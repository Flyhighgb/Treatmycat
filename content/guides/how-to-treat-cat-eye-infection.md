A little clear tear staining is normal for some cats, especially flat-faced breeds like Persians. But a cat with goopy, red, swollen or squinting eyes usually has an eye infection or irritation that needs a vet's help. Eyes can get worse quickly, and some problems that look like a simple infection, such as an ulcer on the surface of the eye, can threaten your cat's sight. The good news is that most eye infections in cats clear up well with the right treatment.

## Signs of an eye problem

- **Discharge**: watery, thick, yellow, green or crusty
- **Redness** of the white of the eye or the inside of the eyelids
- **Squinting** or keeping the eye half closed
- **Swollen eyelids**
- The **third eyelid** (a pale membrane in the inner corner) showing more than usual
- **Pawing or rubbing** at the eye
- A **cloudy**, hazy or bluish look to the eye
- **Sneezing** or a runny nose at the same time

## Common causes

- **Feline herpesvirus (FHV-1).** The most common cause of eye infections in cats. Most cats catch it as kittens, and the virus stays in the body for life. It can flare up again during stress, illness or changes at home.
- **Other infections**, including feline calicivirus, *Chlamydia felis* and *Mycoplasma*. These often come with sneezing and other cold-like signs and spread easily between cats.
- **Corneal ulcers**, scratches or wounds on the clear surface of the eye, often from cat fights, plants or herpesvirus. These are painful and need prompt treatment.
- **Something in the eye**, such as a grass seed, hair or litter dust.
- **Allergies** or irritants like smoke, dust and strong cleaning products.
- **Eyelid problems**, such as eyelids that roll inward (entropion), and **blocked tear ducts**, which are common in flat-faced breeds.
- **Less often**, glaucoma, inflammation inside the eye, or other illnesses that affect the whole body.

## See a vet the same day if

- Your cat is **squinting** or holding an eye **closed**. This usually means pain, often from an ulcer.
- The eye looks **cloudy, blue or hazy**, or has a visible spot or scratch
- The eye is **bulging**, sunken, or looks different in size from the other
- The **pupils are different sizes**
- There's an **injury** to the eye or eyelid, or bleeding in the eye
- Your cat seems to have **trouble seeing**, bumps into things, or is very sensitive to light
- A **kitten's** eyelids are swollen or sealed shut with discharge
- Your cat is also **not eating**, lethargic, or has trouble breathing

Eye problems can get worse within hours, so it's better to have even a mild-looking eye checked than to wait.

## What your vet will do

Your vet will examine both eyes and usually put a harmless orange dye (fluorescein) in the eye to look for ulcers. They may also check the eye pressure and take a swab. Treatment depends on the cause. It can include antibiotic or antiviral eye drops or ointment, oral medicine, or removal of something stuck in the eye. Give every dose exactly as your vet prescribes, and finish the full course, even if the eye looks better.

## How to help at home

These steps support your vet's treatment. They don't replace it.

1. **Clean the eyes gently.** Dampen a soft cotton pad ([see cotton rounds](amazon:soft cotton rounds)) with warm (not hot) water or the saline your vet recommends. Wipe from the inner corner outward, once, and use a **fresh pad for each eye** so you don't spread infection.
2. **Soften crusts first.** Hold a warm, damp pad against dried discharge for a few seconds before wiping. Never pick crusts off.
3. **Give eye drops calmly.** Wrap your cat loosely in a towel, tilt its head back slightly, and drop the medicine onto the eye without touching the tip to the eye. Follow with a treat. If you're giving more than one eye medicine, ask your vet how long to wait between them.
4. **Keep things calm.** Stress can trigger herpesvirus flare-ups. Keep routines predictable and give your cat quiet hiding spots and gentle daily play (see our guide to [the best toys for indoor cats](/guides/best-toys-for-indoor-cats/)).
5. **Keep infected cats apart** from other cats where you can, and wash your hands after handling them. Many eye infections spread between cats.
6. **Keep your cat indoors** until its eyes have healed, away from dust, smoke and strong smells.

## Never

- **Use human eye drops**, especially "redness relief" drops. They can harm cats' eyes.
- **Use leftover eye medicine** from another pet or an old prescription. Some eye drops contain steroids, which can make an ulcer much worse and even lead to losing the eye.
- **Use tea bags, chamomile, breast milk or other home remedies** in the eye. They aren't sterile and can make infections worse.
- **Use essential oils** near your cat's face. They can irritate the eyes and are toxic to cats.
- **Wait more than a day** if the eye is squinting, cloudy or getting worse.

## Common questions

### Can a cat's eye infection go away on its own?

Very mild irritation sometimes settles in a day or two. But many eye problems, especially ulcers, get worse without treatment, and they can look the same as a simple infection. Have your vet check any eye that is goopy, red or squinting.

### Is my cat's eye infection contagious to my other cats?

Often, yes. Herpesvirus, calicivirus and *Chlamydia* spread between cats through discharge, shared bowls and grooming. Keep the sick cat separate if you can and wash your hands after touching it.

### Can I catch an eye infection from my cat?

Most cat eye infections don't spread to people. *Chlamydia felis* can, rarely, cause conjunctivitis in people, so wash your hands after cleaning your cat's eyes or giving drops.

### Why does my cat's eye infection keep coming back?

Usually because of feline herpesvirus, which stays in the body and can flare up during stress or illness. Your vet can help you manage flare-ups and reduce stress at home.

### Are tear stains on my cat's face normal?

Mild, clear tear staining is common in flat-faced breeds and usually harmless. Wipe it gently each day. See your vet if the discharge becomes thick, colored or smelly, or if the eye looks red or sore.

## The short version

Goopy, red or swollen eyes usually need a vet, and squinting, cloudiness or an injury need one the same day. At home, gently wipe the eyes with a fresh warm, damp pad for each eye, give medicine exactly as prescribed, and never use human or leftover eye drops.
