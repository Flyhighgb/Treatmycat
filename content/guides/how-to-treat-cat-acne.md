If your cat's chin looks dirty, like it's covered in coffee grounds or tiny black specks that don't brush off, it's probably feline acne. It's common, it can affect cats of any age, and mild cases are usually more of a cosmetic issue than a health one. But acne can become infected and sore, and several other skin problems look similar, so it's worth having your vet take a look, especially the first time you notice it.

## What cat acne looks like

- **Blackheads** (small black specks) on the chin and sometimes the lower lip
- A chin that looks **dirty or gritty**, even after cleaning
- **Small red bumps or pimples**, sometimes with white heads
- **Swelling**, crusting or scabs in more severe cases
- **Hair loss** on the chin
- Your cat **rubbing or scratching** its chin more than usual

## Why it happens

Cats have oil glands on their chin and lips. Acne happens when hair follicles there become clogged with oil and debris, forming blackheads. Vets don't fully understand why some cats get it and others don't, but common factors include:

- **Not grooming the chin well.** Cats can't easily reach their own chin, and older or overweight cats groom less.
- **Food bowls.** Plastic bowls scratch easily and can harbor bacteria. Some cats improve when switched to stainless steel or ceramic, although the link isn't proven.
- **Food residue** left on the chin after meals, especially wet food.
- **Stress** and a weaker immune system.
- **Other skin conditions**, such as allergies, which can make acne worse.

Once follicles are clogged, bacteria or yeast can move in and cause a **secondary infection**, which makes the chin red, swollen and painful.

## Conditions that look like acne

Your vet will want to rule these out, because they need different treatment:

- **Ringworm**, a fungal infection that can spread to people and other pets
- **Mites**, such as *Demodex*
- **Flea allergy** or food allergy
- **Eosinophilic granuloma complex**, an immune reaction that can cause a swollen chin ("fat chin")
- **Flea dirt** on the chin, which can look like blackheads (see our guide to [treating fleas](/guides/how-to-treat-cat-fleas/))

## Home care for mild acne

This is for a cat with a few blackheads and no redness, swelling or pain, ideally after your vet has confirmed it's acne.

1. **Switch to stainless steel or ceramic bowls** for food and water ([see stainless steel cat bowls](amazon:stainless steel cat bowls shallow)). Shallow, wide bowls keep the chin cleaner.
2. **Wash bowls every day** with hot water and mild dish soap, and rinse well.
3. **Wipe your cat's chin after meals** with a soft cloth dampened with warm water, then pat it dry.
4. **Use only products your vet recommends.** Your vet may suggest a gentle antiseptic wipe or cleanser made for cats. Use it as directed, and stop if the skin looks more irritated.
5. **Help with grooming.** Gently comb older or overweight cats, and work with your vet on weight loss if needed (see our guide to [healthy treats](/guides/healthy-cat-treats/) for keeping treats in check).
6. **Be patient.** Mild acne can take several weeks to clear, and some cats have it on and off for life. The goal is to keep it mild and comfortable.

## Never

- **Squeeze or pick** at blackheads or pimples. It's painful, pushes infection deeper and can cause scarring.
- **Use human acne products**, such as benzoyl peroxide, salicylic acid or medicated face washes, unless your vet specifically says so. Many are too strong for cats, and cats lick their faces, so they swallow what's put there.
- **Use tea tree or other essential oils.** They can be toxic to cats.
- **Use alcohol or hydrogen peroxide** on the chin. They sting and damage the skin.
- **Shave the chin yourself.** If clipping is needed, your vet will do it safely.

## See a vet if

- The chin is **red, swollen, hot or painful**
- There's **pus, bleeding or crusting**
- You see **hair loss**, especially round, scaly patches
- The bumps are **spreading** beyond the chin and lips
- Your cat won't let you touch its chin, or is **eating less**
- Mild acne hasn't improved after **2 to 3 weeks** of home care

Your vet may take a skin scraping, hair sample or swab to check for mites, ringworm or infection. Treatment depends on the cause and may include clipping the area, medicated washes or wipes, and prescription medication if there's an infection, given exactly as your vet prescribes.

## Common questions

### Is cat acne contagious?

Acne itself isn't contagious to people or other pets. But some conditions that look like acne, such as ringworm, can spread, which is one reason to have your vet check it.

### Can cat acne go away on its own?

Mild acne sometimes clears up, especially once the cause (like a plastic bowl) is removed. But it often comes and goes. Keeping the chin and bowls clean helps prevent flare-ups.

### Do plastic bowls really cause cat acne?

They haven't been proven to cause it, but plastic scratches easily and can hold bacteria. Switching to stainless steel or ceramic is cheap and helps some cats, so it's worth trying.

### Can I use coconut oil on my cat's chin acne?

It's best not to. Oils can clog follicles further, and your cat will lick it off. Ask your vet for a cat-safe cleanser instead.

### What does infected cat acne look like?

The chin becomes red, swollen and sore, and you may see pus, crusts or bleeding. Your cat may resist being touched there. Infected acne needs a vet visit.

## The short version

Cat acne shows up as blackheads on the chin. Switch to stainless steel or ceramic bowls, wash them daily, and gently wipe your cat's chin after meals. Never squeeze spots or use human acne products. Redness, swelling, pus, hair loss or no improvement after a few weeks all mean a trip to the vet.
