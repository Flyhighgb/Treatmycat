A cat that needs to lose weight should do it slowly, with a measured amount of the right food, fewer treats and more play, and ideally with a plan from your vet. Cutting food sharply is the one thing not to do. A cat that eats too little can develop fatty liver disease (hepatic lipidosis), which is life-threatening, so for cats slow and steady really is the only safe route.

## Is your cat actually overweight?

Vets use a **body condition score**, usually on a 1 to 9 scale where 5 is ideal. You can do a rough check at home:

- **Ribs:** run your hands along your cat's sides. You should feel the ribs easily under a thin layer of fat, without pressing hard.
- **Waist:** look down from above. There should be a gentle inward curve behind the ribs.
- **Belly:** from the side, the belly should tuck up a little. A small loose flap of skin low on the belly (the "primordial pouch") is normal; a round, firm belly is not.

If you can't feel the ribs, can't see a waist, or your cat struggles to groom its back, it is probably carrying too much weight. Your vet can score it properly and work out a target weight.

## Why it matters

Extra weight makes cats more likely to develop diabetes, arthritis, urinary problems and skin trouble from being unable to groom. Overweight cats tend to be less active, which makes it worse. Losing even part of the extra weight can make a real difference: some diabetic cats need less treatment once they slim down, and stiff cats often move more easily. If your cat is already stiff, our guide to [arthritis in cats](/guides/how-to-help-cat-with-arthritis/) covers ways to make life easier while it loses weight.

## A weight-loss plan that works

1. **Start with a vet visit.** Your vet can check for health problems that affect weight or exercise, such as arthritis, and calculate how many calories your cat should eat to lose weight safely.
2. **Choose the right food.** Prescription or veterinary weight-loss diets are high in protein, which helps keep muscle, and often higher in fiber and water, which help cats feel full. Simply feeding less of a regular food can leave a cat hungry and short of nutrients.
3. **Measure every meal.** Scoops and cups are surprisingly inaccurate. Weigh dry food on a small kitchen scale in grams. [See small kitchen scales](amazon:digital kitchen scale grams small).
4. **Split the daily amount into several small meals.** Cats naturally eat little and often. Several measured portions feel like more food than one big one.
5. **Cut back on treats, don't ban them.** Count them as part of the daily allowance and keep them under 10% of calories. Some kibble taken from the day's ration makes a good treat. Our guide to [healthy cat treats](/guides/healthy-cat-treats/) lists low-calorie options.
6. **Make food work.** Puzzle feeders and treat balls make a cat move and think for its food, and slow down fast eaters. [See cat puzzle feeders](amazon:cat puzzle feeder).
7. **Play every day.** Two or three short sessions with a wand toy, chasing and pouncing, add activity without stress. See our [guide to toys for indoor cats](/guides/best-toys-for-indoor-cats/) for ideas.
8. **Get everyone on board.** One person feeding "extras" undoes the plan. Agree on who feeds the cat and when.
9. **In multi-cat homes**, feed cats in separate rooms, pick up bowls after meals, or use a microchip feeder that only opens for the right cat.
10. **Weigh your cat every 2 to 4 weeks** at home or at the clinic, and adjust the amount with your vet if weight isn't moving or is coming off too fast.

Expect it to take months, not weeks. Slow loss is the safe kind, and it is more likely to stay off.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Crash diets or skipping meals.** Never cut food drastically or let a cat go without eating. This is how fatty liver disease starts.
- **Free-feeding a full bowl** and hoping your cat self-regulates.
- **Feeding to stop begging.** Many cats ask for food when they really want attention. Try play or a brushing session instead.
- **Human food scraps**, cheese and milk, which add calories quickly.
- **Diet pills or supplements** without your vet's advice.

## See a vet if

- Your cat **stops eating** or eats much less for a day or more during the diet. Call your vet the same day, especially for an overweight cat.
- It is **losing weight without trying**, or losing it faster than your vet planned
- It is drinking or peeing much more than usual (a possible sign of diabetes or kidney disease)
- It vomits, seems lethargic, or the whites of its eyes or gums look yellow
- It seems stiff, sore or reluctant to jump

## Common questions

### How fast should my cat lose weight?
Slowly. Vets usually aim for around 1% of body weight a week, and sometimes less. For a 15 lb (6.8 kg) cat, that's only a few ounces a week. Your vet will set the right pace for your cat.

### Is wet or dry food better for weight loss?
Both can work if the amount is measured and the calories are right. Wet food contains more water, which can help some cats feel full. A veterinary weight-loss diet in either form is the most reliable option.

### Can I just feed my cat less of its normal food?
It's better not to. Cutting a regular food far enough to cause weight loss can also cut protein, vitamins and minerals below what your cat needs, and leaves it hungry. A food designed for weight loss avoids that.

### Why is my cat still gaining weight on a diet?
Common reasons are hidden extras (treats, another cat's food, a family member feeding scraps), measuring by eye, or very low activity. If none of those apply, ask your vet to check for medical causes.
